## So… about that garden

So I opened my learning garden the other day and the first thing I saw wasn't a note. It was a date. The last time I'd tended anything was 26 March 2026, and that number hit harder than any failing test ever has.

If you've ever kept a journal, a gym streak, or a side project, you know the feeling. The thing doesn't yell at you. It just sits there, quietly being proof that you stopped.

This post is me being honest about that. What a learning garden is, why I think every maker (and yes, every software engineer) should keep one, what mine gave me, and what happened when I walked away from it for a bit.

## What a learning garden actually is

A blog is a stream. You publish a post, it's done, and it slides down the page as newer stuff lands on top. A garden is different. Notes get planted small, revisited, rewritten, linked to each other, and they grow over time. Nothing is ever really "finished" (which, honestly, is the most developer thing ever).

The idea isn't mine. Mike Caulfield's essay [The Garden and the Stream](https://hapgood.us/2015/10/17/the-garden-and-the-stream-a-technopastoral/) is where a lot of people, me included, first ran into it. The short version: the stream is about what's new, the garden is about what's connected.

Mine lives at [garden.developingdvlpr.com](https://garden.developingdvlpr.com). It holds my Rolodex of terms, the concepts I'm working through, and the half-formed notes that eventually become blog posts and talks.

## Why makers (and engineers) should keep one

I spent years as a middle school science and literacy teacher before I wrote a line of production code. One thing teaching drilled into me: you don't really know something until you can explain it to someone else. A garden is that test, running all the time, in public.

For anyone who builds things, it does three jobs:

- **It's memory outside your head.** We touch a ridiculous number of tools, terms and patterns. You will forget most of them. A garden means you only have to learn them once.
- **It's proof of work.** Commits show what you shipped. A garden shows how you think and how you got there.
- **It's your flywheel.** It's where [Learn, Build, Teach](/blog/the-developers-flywheel-learn-build-teach) actually happens, not just a nice idea on a slide.

You don't need to be an expert to keep one. That's kinda the whole point (more on that in a sec…).

**Understanding.** To write a note well, I had to actually understand the thing. Not "I watched a video and nodded" understand. The kind where I can put it in my own words and get the message across. ## Then I stopped tending it

Here's the honest part. I didn't abandon the garden because I got busy (well, not *only* that). I stopped because I started feeling incompetent as a developer.

The good ole imposter syndrome crept in, and it brought a very specific question with it: *why do I think I should keep writing about this?* Smarter, way more competent people have already covered every topic I'd ever plant. Who needs my note on reactivity when the people who built the framework wrote the docs?

So I stopped. And then the shame showed up, which is its own fun little loop. The longer the garden sat untended, the more it felt like evidence that I wasn't the developer I was pretending to be. The less I wanted to open it. The longer it sat. Rinse, repeat.

What I missed in that loop: the garden was never supposed to be the best explanation on the internet. It was supposed to be *my* explanation. That's the part nobody else can write.

## What I reaped anyway

Surprise, surprise: even untended, the garden kept paying me back. The understanding I built while writing those notes didn't disappear when I stopped writing. The competency was still there. The only thing that faded was the accountability, and that turned out to be the piece that kept the other two growing.

That's the lesson I'm taking with me. A garden isn't a performance review. It's a practice. Progress over perfection applies here too: a scrappy note I actually write beats the perfect one I talk myself out of.

But coming back made something else obvious. The way my garden was set up (seedling, budding, evergreen) didn't fit how I actually learn and build as a developer. It had no honest way to say "I stepped away from this" without it feeling like failure.
