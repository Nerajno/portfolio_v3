## Six months of nothing

In [part 1](/blog/why-i-keep-a-learning-garden) I wrote about the six months my garden sat untended, from 26 March 2026 until this October, and why I stopped: imposter syndrome showed up with a very specific question attached, *why do I think I should keep writing about this?* Smarter people had covered every topic I'd ever plant.

But coming back made something else obvious, and it's the part I hadn't seen before. Some of the problem wasn't me. Some of it was the model I'd built the garden on. The standard way of running a digital garden had no honest way to say "I stepped away from this," so six months of absence rendered as six months of failure.

That's a design flaw, not a character flaw. This post is about the flaw. The next one is about what I built instead.

## What a garden is for in the first place

[Part 1](/blog/why-i-keep-a-learning-garden) covers what a learning garden is and the three things mine gave me: understanding, accountability and competency.

Here's what matters for the rest of this post: those three things are not the same kind of thing. Understanding is something I hold. Competency is something I can show. And the standard way of running a garden only knows how to track one of them.

## The standard model, and the thing it quietly assumes

Almost every digital garden on the internet runs the same three stages: **seedling**, **budding**, **evergreen**. Mine did too, for years.

It's a lovely model. It's also an ornamental one, and I mean that literally. Look at what it measures: how developed is this note? That's the whole axis. A seedling is immature, an evergreen is mature, and the implied destination is a thing that stays beautiful indefinitely without you doing much to it.

That's a flower bed. You plant it for the look of it. Success is that it keeps looking good.

And if your garden is a commonplace book — quotes, observations, essays, ideas you turn over — that model fits perfectly. Nothing I'm about to say is a criticism of it in that context.

But I'm not growing a commonplace book. I'm growing the raw material for work I have to do: code I'll ship, talks I'll give, interviews I'll sit in. My garden has a yield. And the moment you're growing for yield, an ornamental model starts getting things wrong.

## Four places it broke for me

**It can't tell understanding from shipping.** A note on how Vue reactivity works and a log of a project I actually shipped both end up tagged "evergreen." Those are completely different wins. One means I could teach it. The other means someone used it. Collapsing them into one word throws away the more interesting half.

**"Evergreen" sounds finished.** Nothing in software is finished. Frameworks change, I change, my understanding changes. An evergreen note is a note I've stopped being suspicious of, which is exactly when it starts quietly going stale.

**There's no honest state for stepping away.** This is the one that cost me six months. When I stopped tending, every note just froze at whatever stage it was in. A seedling from March looked identical to a seedling from yesterday. The system had no vocabulary for *resting* — so all of it read as neglect, including the parts that were simply done and fine.

**There's no honest state for stopping on purpose.** Sometimes I kill a project. Sometimes I decide a topic isn't worth my time. The standard model treats that as an abandoned seedling, when it's actually a decision worth recording — and usually the one with the most to teach.

Notice that the last two are the same hole: the model only describes growth. Everything that isn't growth looks like failure. If you've ever felt guilty opening your own notes, that's probably not your discipline. That's your taxonomy.

## Flowers or food

So I went back to the metaphor and asked how actual gardeners think about this. Not metaphor-gardeners. People with dirt under their nails.

Turns out flower gardens and food gardens share almost all of their early stages and then split at the very end. Both get planted, both put out leaves, both bud. Then flowers **bloom**, and crops bear **fruit**.

That's not a cosmetic difference. It's a difference in what the plant is *for*, and it changes everything downstream:

|  | Flower garden | Food garden |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grown for | How it looks | What it yields |
| Success is | It keeps blooming | You ate it |
| The end state | Ongoing display | A thing you carried away |
| When you stop | It fades, sadly | The season ended, normally |

And that split maps almost exactly onto what a developer's garden actually grows:

- **Concepts** are things I come to understand. Those *bloom*. The payoff is that I could teach it, and the note stays on display because other people read it.
- **Projects** are things I build and ship. Those bear *fruit*. The payoff is that it works and someone used it, and then the season is over.

One standard model was being asked to cover both, which is why it kept producing "evergreen" for two things that have nothing in common.

It also explains the guilt. A flower bed that stops blooming is dying. A vegetable patch in November isn't dying — it's between seasons. Six months of untended project notes was never neglect. I just had a model that could only read it one way.

## Not everything starts as a seed

Here's the other thing the standard model glosses over, and it's the one that actually helped me most.

Real gardeners don't grow everything from seed. Some plants start as a sucker pulled off a neighbour's plant and passed over the fence. Some get grafted onto sturdier rootstock. My notes work exactly the same way.

| How it got in | In the garden | In my learning garden |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Seed** | Grown from scratch | An idea or term I started cold |
| **Transplant** | A cutting from someone else's plant, rooted in your soil | Something I picked up from a talk, a mentor, a course or a coworker, then rewrote in my own words |
| **Graft** | New growth joined onto established rootstock | New knowledge joined onto something I already know well — Nuxt on top of Vue 3 |

Origin isn't a stage. It's a fact about where a note came from, so it gets its own field rather than a chip. But it does change where a note starts: seeds and transplants begin at the bottom, while a graft can start further along, because the rootstock is doing the heavy lifting.

And here's why this one matters more than it looks. Go back to the question imposter syndrome kept asking me: *smarter people already covered this, so why am I writing it?*

Most of my garden is transplanted. Of course someone said it first. That's where the cutting came from. Nobody stands in a garden full of cuttings from the neighbourhood and calls it fraudulent — that's just how gardens get built. The work was never originating the plant. The work is getting it to take root in my soil.

The standard model has no word for that, so for six months I didn't have one either.

## Decide what you're growing for

The point isn't that the standard model is wrong. It's that it encodes a purpose, and most of us inherited it without checking whether that purpose was ours.

So before you copy anyone's stages, including mine, answer the question underneath them: what is this garden *for*?

- Growing for display, for the pleasure of turning ideas over, for a commonplace book you'll reread in ten years? Seedling, budding, evergreen is genuinely good. Keep it.
- Growing for yield — material you'll ship, teach, interview on, build with? You need a model that can say it bore fruit, and one that can say the season ended without calling it a failure.

Gardeners have a word for what sits underneath that question: **sustenance**. Not the harvest, but what the harvest feeds. Mine feeds my own work first, which is the honest order, and then whoever finds a note useful enough to take a cutting from. A garden grown for sustenance gets tended in the weeks nobody is watching, because the person it feeds is you.

Most developers I know are growing food and tagging it like flowers. Then they feel bad when the bed goes quiet in November.

What I've landed on is both tracks, running side by side, with a field that says which one a note is on. That's [the next post](/blog/how-i-actually-run-my-learning-garden): the stages, the front matter, how the two tracks split across my two sites, and the parts I'm still not sure about.

Before then, I'd like to know how you handle this. If you keep a garden, what do you do with the notes you've stepped away from? Hide them, or let them rest in public?
