*In The Middle — Episode 0: why the stretch between junior and senior needs its own map.*

Before I wrote code for a living, I taught middle school.

If you've never been in a middle school classroom, here's the short version: everybody in the room is in between. Too old for the things that worked last year. Not quite ready for what's coming next. Every day, you're trying to figure out which version of yourself shows up.

I didn't expect to land back in the middle. But here I am.

In February, I called myself a "Competent\~ish Mid-Level Developer" in my year-in-review. In October, my title changed to Software Engineer. And the honest truth? The title changed faster than the feeling did.

> **\[Your moment goes here\]** One specific time when knowing the syntax wasn't the skill — a code review, a scoping call, a bug where you knew *how* to fix it but not *whether* you should. Two or three sentences. That's the hook.

That moment is the whole series in miniature. The code wasn't the hard part. The judgment was.

## So What Is "The Middle"?

The middle isn't a job title. It isn't a number of years, either. I've met two-year devs who are firmly in it and eight-year devs who never got there.

Here's how I define it:

> **The middle is the gap between "I can make it work" and "I know why it should work this way."**

The imposter syndrome questions I wrote about in [Skills to Bills](https://developingdvlpr.com/blog/skills-to-bills-demonstrated-competency) map onto it pretty cleanly:

```
Junior:  "Do I know it?"
   └──> Middle:  "Can I build it reliably — and choose well?"
           └──> Senior:  "Can I communicate, defend, and sell it?"
```

A junior's job is to learn the tools. A senior's job is to make calls that other people trust. The middle is where you build the instinct that makes those calls possible:

- Noticing a code smell before you can name it
- Knowing which tool fits *this* problem, not just the one you know best
- Reviewing someone else's PR and leaving a comment that actually helps
- Saying "that's a three-week job, not a three-day job" — and being right

None of those are syntax. All of them are judgment.

## Why Nobody Writes About the Middle

Go look at what's out there for developers. You'll find two piles.

**Pile one: getting started.** Bootcamps, "build a todo app," your first framework, your first job. I've written plenty of these myself — my whole Vue 3 Fundamentals series lives here. It's needed, and it's crowded.

**Pile two: already senior.** Staff engineer essays, architecture deep dives, org design. Great stuff. But it assumes you already have the context, the vocabulary, and the scar tissue.

The middle falls through the crack between them.

This is the teacher in me talking, but it's a curriculum problem. When I taught, the hardest grades to plan for weren't the beginners or the ones about to graduate. They were the ones in between — where the fundamentals were *supposed* to be in place, and the goal shifted from learning facts to learning how to think with them.

Most developers cross the middle by accident. A good mentor here. A brutal code review there. One production incident that taught more than a year of tutorials.

That works — eventually. But it's slow, and it depends a lot on luck. **This series is my attempt to make the path visible on purpose.**

## "But Doesn't AI Make This Irrelevant?"

Fair question. If an assistant can write the function, why spend time on the middle at all?

Here's my take: **practices change; principles don't.**

AI tools have changed *how* we write code. They haven't changed what good code is, why a schema should be normalized, or what makes an API safe to call twice. If anything, the middle matters more now:

- **More code, faster** means more code to review. Code review is a middle skill.
- **Plausible-looking output** means you need the instinct to spot what's off. That instinct comes from fundamentals.
- **Infinite options** means picking the right tool matters more. Tool selection is a judgment call.
- **Learning on demand** is easier than ever — but just-in-time learning only works if you have enough foundation to know what to look up.

The assistant can hand you an answer. The middle is where you learn to tell whether it's the *right* one.

## How This Works (and Where You Come In)

I'm not writing this from the top of the mountain. I'm writing it from the middle of the climb. So I'm not doing it alone.

Every episode follows the same four-part format:

1. **The Concept** — what it is, in plain language
2. **The Gap** — where mid-level devs usually get stuck on it
3. **Ask the Room** — input from other developers on how *they* learned it

## Final Thoughts

The middle isn't a waiting room between junior and senior. It's where the real work of becoming an engineer happens — turning what you know into judgment you can trust.

I'm in it right now. New title, same questions, plenty left to learn. That's the point. Progress over perfection applies to careers, not just side projects.

Here's what this series will try to do:

- Name the skills that separate "it works" from "it's right"
- Pull in voices beyond mine, so it's a map built by the people who walked the road
- Keep the fundamentals front and center, even as the tools keep changing
