# Progress Over Perfection: My 2025 Journey from Junior to Competent Mid-Level Developer

## Why I Do This ?

So I am having a chat with a giant in the field, know for their zines and awesone docuemtation in the React space, the question was posed to me - "Why do you do this ?". I know it was at DevOps Days 2025 (see [my talk](/blog/DevOpsDays2025)), I don't remember if was before or after I gave my talk but we went on talk about the future of the web, the AI landscape and the direction of the tech community. This question, however, stood out in my mind for the rest of the year because essentially, it was the same question I asked myself from time to time  and its all the definition of my "Why" ?  The honest answer? Because I needed to prove to myself that I could and because no-one was willing to have this part of the tech conversation.

2025 was supposed to be the year I "became" a mid-level developer. Spoiler alert: that's not how it works and in alot of ways I did and I didn't. I got into this field as it has been chasing me for about 20 years before I accepted my fate as a technilogist (I wanted to be a professor at the intersection of applied tech, literacy and science education working specifically with middle schoolers, I even went to MICO and was a tenured teacher). Unfortunately, there was no magical moment where I levelled up like in a video game. Instead, it's been a year of building things that actually work, learning from things that broke ( there was a lot of that), speaking at conferences even when I felt underqualified, and slowly proving—to myself —that I belong in this field. We shall be positive about this and not have the conversation about "homogenization thinking" of the tech community.  So in the wonder spirit of writing more as a software developer, here is my 2025 recap —the projects that taught me, the talks that pushed me, the mental shifts that stuck, and yeah, the stuff I'm still figuring out.


## Code That Counted

Let's start with what I actually built this year, because if you're not shipping, you're just planning.

### KitchenOnWheels: My First Real Commercial Application

The biggest technical project I tackled was **KitchenOnWheels**, a commercial kitchen equipment rental platform. This was my first time owning a project from architecture decisions to deployment—not just implementing features someone else designed, but making the actual calls about how the whole thing should work.

Working with Vue.js at this scale taught me the difference between building tutorial apps and building real applications. I had to think about state management patterns (choosing between Pinia and Vuex wasn't just theoretical anymore), structure composables so I could actually reuse code, and care about bundle size because it affected real users. Every decision had consequences I'd have to live with later.

The technical challenge wasn't just writing code that worked—it was **writing code I could maintain and extend** when requirements changed (and they always changed). I made mistakes. I refactored things I wish I'd gotten right the first time. But I shipped it, it works, and people are using it. That feels like a mid-level milestone to me.

### Vue Mastery Content: Learning by Teaching

One of my favorite projects this year was working through Vue Mastery course content and turning what I learned into blog articles. I wasn't an expert teaching beginners—I was a junior developer learning out loud and documenting what actually helped me understand complex concepts.

Here's what I discovered: **writing about something you just learned forces you to actually understand it**. You can't fake your way through an explanation when someone might read your article at 2 AM trying to fix their broken app. Creating content like "11 JavaScript Fundamentals for Vue Developers" meant I had to really internalize the fundamentals—not just copy-paste code until it worked.

The feedback loop is powerful: people read my posts, ask questions, and their questions reveal gaps in my own understanding. Then I go learn more, update my mental models, and write better explanations. This series became popular not because I'm an expert, but because I'm learning alongside my readers and explaining things in ways that made sense to me when I was stuck.

### Agent-Based Code Review: Exploring New Tools

I spent time this year exploring **agent-based code review systems**—essentially using AI to help catch bugs and suggest improvements. This wasn't production-critical work, but it taught me about developer workflows and automation in ways I hadn't experienced before.

The interesting part wasn't just getting the tools working—it was learning to think about *when* automation helps and *when* you actually need human eyes on code. That judgment is something I'm still developing, but experimenting with these tools pushed me to think beyond just "can I make this work?" to "should this work this way?"

I'm learning that mid-level developers don't just consume tools—they evaluate tools, understand their trade-offs, and make informed decisions about when to use them. I'm not there yet, but I'm building that muscle.

### Blog Infrastructure: The Unsexy Learning Opportunities

I also shipped infrastructure work on my Astro blog: view counters, **Generative Engine Optimization (GEO)** techniques, and portfolio architecture refactoring. These weren't flashy features, but they taught me about performance optimization, SEO, and the kind of infrastructure work that makes real applications reliable.

Here's what I learned: **caring about the boring stuff is what separates developers who just make things work from developers who make things work well**. View counters taught me about database queries and analytics. GEO taught me about content strategy for AI-first search. Refactoring taught me about technical debt and when it's worth paying it down.

These projects pushed me beyond my comfort zone of component-building into the deeper technical work that mid-level developers are expected to handle. It's one thing to implement a feature; it's another to make it performant, maintainable, and discoverable.

## From Attendee to Speaker: Finding My Voice

If 2024 was about attending conferences and soaking up knowledge in Atlanta's tech community, 2025 was about **stepping up to share what I was learning** through speaking engagements.

### Carolina Codes 2025: My First Real Conference Talk

Speaking at **Carolina Codes Conference** in September was terrifying. Not "butterflies in stomach" nervous—legitimately "what am I doing here" terrified. This wasn't a local meetup where I knew half the people. This was a real conference with experienced developers who would see right through me if I didn't know my stuff.

My talk, "Getting Your Side Projects Across the Finish Line," came from a very personal place: I was struggling to finish my own projects and had developed a framework that was actually helping. The Kill List approach wasn't some methodology I'd perfected over years—it was something I'd literally used the previous month to ship KitchenOnWheels.

The response surprised me. People came up afterward with questions, thanked me for the practical advice, asked if I had more content. One person said my framework "finally made project management make sense." That validation meant everything because it proved **you don't have to be an expert to help people—you just have to be a few steps ahead and willing to share what worked for you**.

### The Speaking Circuit: Building Confidence Through Reps

I continued speaking at Atlanta events like **DevNexus** and **Connect.Tech**, each talk building on the last. My topics evolved from "here's what I'm learning" to "here's what I've learned and applied," which felt like progress even if I still got nervous before every single talk.

Each speaking engagement taught me something important: **preparation is the antidote to imposter syndrome**. When I knew my material cold—not because I was an expert but because I'd lived it, struggled with it, and figured it out—I could answer questions confidently. When I hadn't prepared enough, the questions exposed my gaps fast.

Speaking pushed me to go deeper than I would have otherwise. You can't vaguely understand something when you're standing in front of people who might ask "why?" or "what about this edge case?" That pressure forced me to actually learn, not just memorize. And honestly? Getting better at explaining things made me better at understanding them in the first place.

## Mid-Level Mindset: How My Thinking Changed

The technical work and speaking opportunities were visible progress, but the real growth happened in **how I started thinking about development work differently than I did as a complete beginner**.

### From "Make It Work" to "Make It Work Well"

When I started out, my entire focus was **getting code to work at all**. If it compiled and the feature functioned, I considered it done. In 2025, I started thinking beyond just working code to *how* the code works and *why* I made certain choices.

This shift shows up everywhere: I'm not just using Pinia because the tutorial said to; I'm thinking about whether my app's state management needs justify the complexity. I'm not just writing components; I'm thinking about component APIs and whether other code (including future me) can understand and extend them. I'm starting to care about things like code readability, maintainability, and whether my solutions will make sense three months from now.

That's the mid-level mindset I'm working toward: caring about quality, not just functionality.

### From Perfect to Shipped

For way too long, I had side projects stuck at 80% done because I kept chasing perfection. "Just one more feature." "Just need to refactor this one part." "Just need to make it look better first." Meanwhile, nothing actually shipped.

2025 taught me that **done beats perfect, and shipped beats done**. The Kill List Framework I talked about at Carolina Codes came from finally accepting that my first versions won't be perfect—and that's okay. KitchenOnWheels shipped with features I knew could be better, but it shipped. People are using it. I'm getting feedback. I can iterate based on real usage instead of imagined perfection.

This was hard to learn because, as a junior dev, you're constantly worried about looking incompetent. But I realized: shipping something functional that improves over time is way more impressive than never shipping anything because it's not perfect enough.

### From Struggling Alone to Learning Together

Early in my career, I thought asking for help meant I wasn't good enough. I'd spend hours stuck on a problem that someone with more experience could have solved in five minutes, all because I was too proud or scared to ask.

2025 taught me that **learning to collaborate is a skill as important as any technical skill**. My mentorship work and community involvement showed me that the best developers aren't the ones who know everything—they're the ones who know when to ask questions, how to help others, and how to learn from people around them.

I've started participating more actively: answering questions in Discord servers (even when I'm not 100% sure but can help troubleshoot), reviewing others' code, sharing my own struggles publicly. The feedback loop is incredible: teaching someone else how to debug their component forces me to understand debugging better. Asking smart questions leads to better answers.

Mid-level isn't about knowing everything—it's about knowing how to work effectively with others.

### From Features to Systems (Starting To)

When I started working on KitchenOnWheels, I realized I couldn't just think "how do I build this booking form?" anymore. I had to think "how does this form connect to the rest of the app? What happens if two people try to book the same equipment? How do I handle errors in a way that doesn't break everything?"

I'm not architecting complex distributed systems or anything, but **I'm starting to think beyond individual features**. I'm learning to consider: How does this piece fit with the other pieces? What breaks if this fails? Can someone else understand what I built? Will this be a nightmare to maintain in six months?

This systems thinking is still developing—I catch myself reverting to "just make the feature work" mode all the time—but the awareness is there now. Mid-level developers think about how their code fits into the larger application, and that's the muscle I'm trying to build.

### From Consuming Knowledge to Sharing It

Creating blog content and speaking at conferences taught me that **explaining something is the fastest way to find out if you actually understand it**. When I was just consuming tutorials and courses, I could fool myself into thinking I understood concepts. Writing blog posts exposed every gap in my knowledge instantly.

Writing "11 JavaScript Fundamentals for Vue Developers" forced me to really understand those fundamentals—not just use them, but explain why they matter and when to apply them. Every tutorial I wrote became a test: if I can't explain this clearly, I don't understand it well enough yet.

The unexpected benefit: teaching others accelerated my own learning. People ask questions I hadn't considered. They point out edge cases I missed. They share their own solutions that are sometimes better than mine. By putting myself out there as someone learning and sharing, I created a feedback loop that made me learn faster than I ever did just consuming content alone.

## Real Talk: What I'm Still Working On

Let's be honest about what's still hard, because real growth stories include the messy parts, and the messy parts are what actually help other junior devs know they're not alone.

### Finishing What I Start

I gave a talk about completing side projects, which is hilarious considering I have three half-finished projects on GitHub right now. The QR code tracking system? 75% done. The conference networking platform? Works locally, never deployed. The portfolio refactor? Been "almost done" for two months.

The Kill List Framework helps, but frameworks don't solve the fundamental problem: **when you work full-time and have limited energy, every side project is competing for the same scarce resource—your evening and weekend hours**. Some weeks I'm motivated and productive. Other weeks I'm exhausted from work and can barely think about code.

I've gotten better at this—breaking work into smaller pieces, being realistic about scope, actually defining what "done" means before I start. But I'm not going to pretend I've solved it. The honest truth is that finishing side projects as a junior dev with a full-time job is genuinely difficult, and anyone who says otherwise either has more energy than me or fewer responsibilities.

### The "What Should I Learn Next?" Overwhelm

I'm trying to get really good at Vue.js while also learning TypeScript properly while also keeping up with what's happening in Re (because job postings mention it) while also understanding Node.js better and figuring out Astro and Supabase and testing frameworks and... you get the point.

**The frontend ecosystem moves so fast that I constantly feel like I'm falling behind.** Everyone says "go deep, not wide," but when you're job hunting or trying to prove you're not just a one-trick pony, you feel pressure to know *everything*. And as a junior dev, you don't have the experience yet to know what's actually important versus what's just hype.

I'm trying to be strategic: go deep on Vue since that's what I'm using in production, get competent with TypeScript since that's everywhere now, understand fundamental JavaScript really well since that's the foundation. But I still have FOMO when I see exciting new tools I'm not using yet. I haven't found the perfect balance, and that's something I'm actively working on.

### The "Am I Actually Any Good?" Question

Even after shipping KitchenOnWheels, speaking at conferences, and getting positive feedback on my blog posts, I still have moments—honestly, frequent moments—where I wonder if I'm actually any good at this or if I've just been lucky.

I'll see someone's GitHub profile with contributions to major open source projects and think "I'll never be that good." Or I'll read a technical blog post about some complex system design and barely understand half of it. Or I'll struggle with a bug that I feel like a "real" developer would solve instantly. The voice in my head says "you're a fraud and everyone's about to find out."

The difference this year is that I'm learning to **recognize imposter syndrome for what it is instead of letting it paralyze me**. When I feel like I don't know enough, I remind myself: six months ago I didn't know how to build a full Vue application, and now I've shipped one that people actually use. A year ago I'd never spoken at a conference, and now I've done multiple talks. The gap between what I know and what I want to know is just... the nature of this field. Everyone has that gap, even senior developers.

I'm getting better at sitting with the discomfort of not knowing everything while still being proud of what I've learned.

### Balancing Everything (Badly, Sometimes)

Here's the reality nobody talks about: balancing a full-time job, side projects, blogging, speaking prep, learning new tech, and having an actual life is **really, really hard**. Some weeks I nail it—productive at work, progress on side projects, blog post published, gym sessions hit. Other weeks I'm barely surviving and Netflix is the only thing I have energy for.

- Study design patterns intentionally (not just use them accidentally)
- Look at well-architected open source projects to see how experienced developers structure things

I'm not trying to become a systems architect overnight. I'm trying to **develop the habit of thinking one level above my current feature**. That's what mid-level developers do—they see connections and consequences that beginners miss.
