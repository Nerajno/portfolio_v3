# Perfected Imperfection: My 2025 Journey from Junior to Competent Mid-Level Developer

# Why I Do This: My 2025 Year-in-Review

At DevOps Days 2025, a giant in the React space asked me a simple question: "Why do you do this?"

The honest answer? Because I needed to prove to myself that I could, and because no one was having this part of the tech conversation.

2025 was supposed to be the year I "became" a mid-level developer. Spoiler: that's not how it works. There was no magical level-up moment. Instead, it was a year of building things that actually work, learning from things that broke, speaking at conferences when I felt underqualified, and slowly proving—to myself—that I belong here.

So here's my 2025 recap: the projects that taught me, the talks that pushed me, and the mental shifts that stuck.

## Code That Counted

**KitchenOnWheels: My First Real Commercial Application**

The biggest technical project I tackled was KitchenOnWheels, a commercial kitchen equipment rental platform. This was my first time owning a project from architecture to deployment—not just implementing features someone else designed, but making the actual calls.

Working with Vue.js at scale taught me the difference between tutorial apps and real applications. I had to think about state management (Pinia vs Vuex), structure reusable composables, and care about bundle size. Every decision had consequences I'd have to live with later.

I made mistakes. I refactored things I wish I'd gotten right the first time. But I shipped it, it works, and people are using it. That feels like a mid-level milestone.

**Vue Mastery Content: Learning by Teaching**

My favorite project was working through Vue Mastery courses and turning what I learned into blog articles. I wasn't an expert teaching beginners—I was a junior developer learning out loud.

Here's what I discovered: writing about something you just learned forces you to actually understand it. Creating "11 JavaScript Fundamentals for Vue Developers" meant really internalizing the fundamentals, not just copy-pasting code.

The feedback loop is powerful. People read my posts, ask questions, and their questions reveal gaps in my understanding. I learn more, update my mental models, write better explanations. This series became popular not because I'm an expert, but because I'm learning alongside my readers.

**The Unsexy Stuff That Matters**

I also shipped infrastructure work on my Astro blog: view counters, Generative Engine Optimization (GEO), and portfolio refactoring. Not flashy, but they taught me that caring about the boring stuff separates developers who make things work from developers who make things work *well*.

## From Attendee to Speaker

If 2024 was about attending conferences, 2025 was about stepping up to share what I was learning.

**Carolina Codes 2025**

Speaking at Carolina Codes in September was terrifying. Not "butterflies" nervous—legitimately "what am I doing here" terrified.

My talk, "Getting Your Side Projects Across the Finish Line," came from a personal place: I was struggling to finish my own projects. The Kill List approach wasn't some perfected methodology—it was something I'd literally used the previous month to ship KitchenOnWheels.

The response surprised me. People thanked me for practical advice. One person said my framework "finally made project management make sense." That proved you don't have to be an expert to help people—you just have to be a few steps ahead and willing to share what worked.

I continued speaking at DevNexus and Connect.Tech, each talk building my confidence. Each engagement taught me: preparation is the antidote to imposter syndrome. When I knew my material cold—not because I was an expert but because I'd lived it—I could answer questions confidently.

## How My Thinking Changed

The technical work was visible progress, but the real growth happened in how I think about development.

**From "Make It Work" to "Make It Work Well"**

I started thinking beyond just working code to *how* the code works and *why* I made certain choices. I'm not just using Pinia because the tutorial said to—I'm thinking about whether my app's state management needs justify the complexity. That's the mid-level mindset: caring about quality, not just functionality.

**From Perfect to Shipped**

I had side projects stuck at 80% done because I kept chasing perfection. 2025 taught me that done beats perfect, and shipped beats done. KitchenOnWheels shipped with features I knew could be better—but it shipped. I'm getting feedback. I can iterate based on real usage instead of imagined perfection.

**From Struggling Alone to Learning Together**

Early on, asking for help meant I wasn't good enough. 2025 taught me that collaboration is as important as any technical skill. The best developers aren't the ones who know everything—they're the ones who know when to ask questions and how to learn from others.

**From Features to Systems**

Working on KitchenOnWheels, I couldn't just think "how do I build this form?" I had to think: how does this connect to the rest of the app? What happens if two people book the same equipment? This systems thinking is still developing, but the awareness is there now.

## Real Talk: What's Still Hard

**Finishing What I Start**

I gave a talk about completing projects, which is hilarious considering I have three half-finished projects on GitHub right now. The Kill List Framework helps, but frameworks don't solve the fundamental problem: limited energy competing with full-time work.

I've gotten better—breaking work into smaller pieces, being realistic about scope. But finishing side projects as a junior dev with a full-time job is genuinely difficult.

**The "What Should I Learn Next?" Overwhelm**

The frontend ecosystem moves fast. I constantly feel like I'm falling behind. Everyone says "go deep, not wide," but when you're job hunting, you feel pressure to know everything.

I'm trying to be strategic: go deep on Vue, get competent with TypeScript, understand fundamental JavaScript. But I still have FOMO when I see exciting new tools.

**The "Am I Actually Any Good?" Question**

Even after shipping KitchenOnWheels and speaking at conferences, I still wonder if I'm actually good at this or just lucky. The difference this year is recognizing imposter syndrome for what it is instead of letting it paralyze me.

## What I'm Focused On for 2026

**1. Actually Finish Projects**
Three projects to finish in Q1: QR code tracking system, conference networking platform MVP, portfolio refactor. Done beats perfect.

**2. Keep Learning in Public**
One technical blog post every two weeks. Document wins *and* failures. The failures teach more.

**3. Get Really Good at Vue + TypeScript**
Go deep on this stack instead of chasing every new framework. Complete a comprehensive TypeScript course. Build a complex Vue 3 project. Contribute to open source.

**4. Think Beyond Individual Features**
Develop the habit of thinking one level above my current feature. Before building, sketch connections. Ask "what breaks if this fails?"

**5. Help Others**
Teaching forces better understanding. Be active in Vue/frontend communities. Do informal code review. Keep speaking at conferences.

## The Truth About Leveling Up

After my Carolina Codes talk, that developer said, "That was really helpful. I've been stuck on a project for months and your framework gave me a path forward."

That's when it hit me: I might still feel like a junior developer half the time, but I'm already helping people and shipping real things. That's not nothing.

2025 taught me that leveling up isn't about suddenly feeling confident one day. It's about doing slightly harder things than you're comfortable with, failing sometimes, learning from it, and gradually expanding what you're capable of.

The journey from junior to mid-level isn't a straight line. There's no moment where someone says "congratulations, you're mid-level now." You just wake up one day and realize you can do things you couldn't do a year ago.

I'm not "there" yet—wherever "there" is. But I'm further along than I was. And that's what 2025 was about: making progress, even when it felt messy and uncertain.

So here's to 2025—a year of building, breaking, learning, and slowly becoming the developer I want to be.

And here's to 2026—where I keep going, keep learning, and keep shipping.

Now if you'll excuse me, I have some side projects to actually finish this time.
