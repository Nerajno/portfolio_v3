## Let's be honest about the graveyard

If you have been coding long enough, you have one. A directory called `projects/` — or maybe `sandbox/`, or just a chaotic mess of folders on your desktop — that is essentially a cemetery for your ambitions. Every `README.md` in there was written with pure enthusiasm. Every `npm create` was a promise you made to yourself.

Most of those promises are still waiting.

I'm not judging. I was there too. I *am* there too, in some ways. But a few years ago, I stopped letting the graveyard win quietly. I built something instead: a website called *IsNerandoDoneYet* — and it changed how I think about side projects, shipping, and what it actually means to build in public.

This is the case for why you should build one too. Or better yet, fork mine.

---

## The invisible tax of unfinished work

There is a cognitive load cost that nobody talks about when it comes to unfinished side projects. Every stalled idea living rent-free in your head takes up mental space that could go toward the things you *are* working on. Psychologists call it the Zeigarnik Effect — our brains hold onto incomplete tasks more persistently than completed ones. It is why you remember the one project you didn't ship louder than the three you did.

The result? You are carrying invisible debt. Not financial debt. *Attention debt.* Every unfinished project is a tab left open in your mental browser, draining your RAM.

I noticed this pattern in myself when I sat down one day and tried to count how many projects I had started in the last two years. I lost count somewhere around fifteen. Fifteen! Some had actual users. Some had detailed PRDs. Some had GitHub repos with one commit and a hopeful `initial setup` message.

The kicker? I couldn't remember which ones were actually done, which ones were abandoned, and which ones were technically "in progress" in the loosest possible definition of that phrase. That was the moment I decided something had to change.

---

## Accountability is not a soft skill. It's architecture.

Here is something I say on stage when I talk about developer culture: accountability is not a soft skill. It is architecture. And just like you would not build a production application without some kind of observability layer, you should not build your career without one either.

When you work at a company, accountability is baked into the process. Stand-ups, sprint reviews, Jira tickets, PR reviews — the entire system is designed to make your progress visible. Your team can see what you are working on, what is blocked, and what shipped.

The moment you go solo on a side project, all of that infrastructure vanishes. No one is asking for your update on Friday. No one is waiting for your PR to merge. Just you, your ambition, and an infinite runway with no checkpoints.

That freedom is also a trap.

The research backs this up. Studies on implementation intentions — the planning strategy where you commit to *when* and *where* you'll complete a goal — show that making your commitments specific and observable dramatically increases follow-through. Announcing a deadline publicly is even more effective because it introduces social accountability: you care what people think, and that is okay. That is a feature, not a character flaw.

*IsNerandoDoneYet* is my implementation intention made visible. It is my observability layer for personal projects.

---

## What building in public actually does for you

When I first heard the phrase "build in public," I thought it was about being performative. Tweeting screenshots of code. Posting revenue numbers. That kind of thing.

I was wrong about what it actually does.

Building in public is not primarily a marketing strategy. It is a clarity tool. When you have to articulate the status of a project to an audience — even a small one — you are forced to be honest with yourself in ways you wouldn't be otherwise. "Currently in progress" sounds fine in your head. It sounds a lot different when it is displayed on a public webpage next to a 15% progress bar that hasn't moved in three months.

That discomfort is productive. It is the friction that keeps you honest.

Here's what I have observed since launching the tracker:

**It kills scope creep.** When a project has a public status and a public URL, the question of "is it done?" has a real answer. You can't endlessly add features to avoid shipping it. The tracker makes vagueness expensive.

**It creates unexpected community.** I cannot tell you how many times someone has hit me up at a conference or in a Discord server asking, "Hey, is [project] live yet? I saw it on your site." That question — from a real person — does more for your momentum than any self-imposed deadline ever could.

**It documents your growth.** Looking at past entries on the tracker is like reading a journal of your technical evolution. The stack choices, the project ideas, the things you were ambitious enough to start — that is a record of who you were as a developer. Future you will want that context.

**It separates the ideas from the commitments.** Not every idea deserves your full energy. Having a tracker forces you to decide: is this a "planned" item I'm putting on the list, or is it a daydream I need to let go of? The act of categorizing is the act of deciding.

## The professional case: your tracker is also your brag doc

Here is where this stops being just a side-project productivity hack and starts being a career strategy.

Most developers are terrible at self-advocacy. Not because they lack confidence — but because they lack receipts. When your annual review comes around, or you're updating your resume, or you're preparing for a promotion conversation, you are suddenly trying to reconstruct twelve months of work from memory. That is not a skills problem. That is a documentation problem.

A brag doc solves it. If you have not heard the term, a brag doc — popularized by Julia Evans in her widely-shared essay on the subject — is exactly what it sounds like: a running document where you record your wins, your shipped work, your impact, and your growth. It is not bragging. It is evidence. And more importantly, it is infrastructure.

> **The key insight:** Your public tracker already *is* your brag doc — you just have to extend each "Done" entry by three to five sentences. When a project changes status, that is a career event. Start treating it like one.

---

## Why a brag doc is non-negotiable

Let me give you the concrete picture of why this matters before we get into how to build it.

**During a job search,** your tracker gives you a living portfolio with context. Not just "I built a Vue 3 app" — but "I built a Vue 3 app with Pinia, Clerk auth, and Supabase, shipped in six weeks, 60% feature-complete at launch, public URL available." That specificity is what makes your resume scannable and your interviews conversational instead of interrogative.

**At performance review time,** you are not scrambling to remember what you shipped in Q2. You have a timestamped list with statuses, dates, and documented outcomes. That data is your argument for the raise or the promotion — and you do not have to hope your manager remembered your wins. You bring the receipts yourself.

**When someone asks you to speak at a conference,** your tracker is your abstract factory. Every "Done" entry is a real problem you solved with a real opinion attached to it. CascadiaJS CFP opens? You have eight entries to mine for talk ideas, not a blank page.

**When imposter syndrome hits,** your brag doc is the cure. You cannot argue with a timestamped list of shipped things. The record exists independently of how you feel on a given Tuesday. That is the point.

The key mindset shift is this: your tracker is not just for other people to hold you accountable. It is for *you* to hold the industry accountable for recognizing what you have actually built. You do not get credit for work that is invisible. Make the work visible — then document what it was worth.

## What a brag doc entry actually looks like

The anatomy is simple. Each entry should answer six questions. You do not need a fancy tool — a section in Notion, a private GitHub repo, a plain markdown file, or even a new JSON field in your tracker all work equally well. The format matters less than the habit.

| Field | What to write |
|---|---|
| `project` | Name of the thing you shipped or meaningfully advanced. *Be specific — "Portfolio v3" not "my site."* |
| `quarter` | When it happened. Quarter + year. *Q1 2026. Not "early this year."* |
| `what_i_did` | One to three sentences on what you actually built or shipped. Stack, scope, decisions made. Past tense, active voice. |
| `impact` | The outcome. Numbers where possible. Qualitative where not. Who used it? What changed? What did you learn? *This is the field most people skip. Don't.* |
| `skills_used` | Tech stack + soft skills. *"Vue 3, TypeScript, Pinia, Clerk, Supabase. Scope scoping, writing a PRD solo, shipping under time pressure."* |
| `evidence` | URL, GitHub link, blog post, conference talk, screenshot. At least one link. *If it's not linkable yet, note where it will be.* |

## The trigger: when to write an entry

The hardest part of a brag doc is not the writing — it is the habit of writing it at the right moment. Most people try to write it retrospectively, at the end of the year, from memory. That is where the effort breaks down.

The better approach is to tie the entry to a status change in your tracker. Here is the workflow:

1. **Project moves to "Done" in your tracker.** This is the moment of maximum context. You know exactly what you built, what decisions you made, and what it took to get there.

2. **Open your brag doc immediately.** Markdown file, Notion page, private GitHub repo — wherever you keep it. The tool does not matter. The timing does.

3. **Fill in the six fields.** Project, quarter, what you did, impact, skills used, evidence. This takes ten minutes if you do it now. It takes an hour of reconstructed memory if you wait three months.

4. **Link the brag entry back to your tracker.** Add a `brag_doc_url` field to your project JSON. Now your public tracker and your private career record cross-reference each other. The system is closed.

## How to maintain it without burning out

A brag doc only works if it stays current. Here is a rhythm that does not require heroic effort:

| When | What to do | Time needed |
|---|---|---|
| On ship | Write the entry while context is hot. Fill all six fields. Link to evidence. | 10 min |
| Weekly | Add one-liners for things that moved but didn't ship yet. Stalled? Note why. | 5 min |
| End of quarter | Review the quarter's entries. Add impact data that wasn't available at ship time. Tag entries that could become conference talks or blog posts. | 30 min |
| Before a review | Pull the last 12 months of entries. Group by theme (shipped product, community, growth, speaking). This is your review prep — done. | 20 min |
| Before a job search | Convert entries to resume bullets. Each entry already has what, how, and impact — the three things a good resume bullet needs. | 1–2 hours |

You will notice the total ongoing cost of this system is roughly fifteen minutes a week. The return on that fifteen minutes compounds every time you interview, present, or negotiate compensation. It is the highest-leverage documentation habit a developer can build.

## Q1 2026 is done. What does that mean?

Here is a moment of honesty: Q1 2026 is over as of April 1st. Whether your Q1 went exactly according to plan or whether it looked nothing like what you intended in January, the quarter is closed. It is a fact now.

That is not a reason for anxiety. It is an invitation to document.

Before you write your Q2 goals — and you should have Q2 goals — do a brief audit of Q1. Go through your tracker and ask yourself three questions about each project you touched:

**What shipped?** Not what you worked on. What actually shipped? These are your wins. Write them down somewhere other than your head. This is where your brag doc starts.

**What stalled, and why?** A stall is not a failure if you understand the reason. Was it scope? Competing priorities? A dependency that was out of your control? Naming the cause gives you information you can act on in Q2.

**What changed about your understanding?** Sometimes the biggest output of a project is not the shipped product — it's the knowledge you built. A POC that didn't survive contact with reality still taught you something about the stack, the market, or your own working style. That learning belongs in your brag doc too.

Q1 2026 had ninety days in it. Some of those days were productive. Some weren't. The tracker doesn't judge — it just records. And the record is what makes the rest of the year clearer.

## Progress over perfection is a philosophy, not a platitude

I built this entire site from a philosophy I started with a few years ago: Progress over Perfection. I write it on conference slides. I talk about it in meetup talks. I put it in my Twitter bio. But it only means something if I actually practice it.

A public tracker is how I practice it.

"Done" on *IsNerandoDoneYet* does not mean perfect. It means shipped. It means live. It means I closed the loop on something I said I would do. "In Progress" means I am actively working on it right now, not "theoretically working on it when inspiration strikes." "Stalled" means I am being honest about the reality, not hiding something I abandoned behind a polite silence.

That taxonomy matters. A lot of developers use vague language about their projects because vague language protects your ego. If a project is "in progress," it can never fail. It is just… perpetually becoming. The tracker forces you to give things a real status, and real statuses create real accountability.

Naming the stall is the first step to ending it.

## The technical case: why this pattern scales

From a pure technical standpoint, a project tracker like this one is a great exercise in building something real with minimal scope. The original version is built with Vue 3, TypeScript, and Tailwind. But the pattern is framework-agnostic — I've included a vanilla HTML/CSS/JS version and a full Vue 3 npm project with Vite, Tailwind, composables, and a JSON data layer.

The data structure is simple: a list of projects, each with a name, a description, a status, a progress percentage, and a date. No database required if you don't want one — a JSON file works fine to start. When you're ready to extend into brag doc territory, add a `brag_doc_url` field to each project object and you have a cross-referenced system between your public tracker and your private career record.

The simplicity is intentional. The goal is to lower the activation energy to ship this thing so you can focus on what matters: keeping it updated and making it public.

## Your turn

I want to be direct with you: if you are a developer who has side projects, you need a public accountability page. Not because the internet is owed your progress report. Not because it will make you go viral. But because *you* are owed an honest accounting of what you're working on.

The graveyard grows in the dark. Bringing your projects into the light — even just a simple web page with a list and some status badges — changes the relationship you have with your own ambitions. And when you extend that tracker into a running brag doc using the six-field format, you build something more durable: a private career record that you control, that is always current, and that is ready to become a resume bullet, a conference abstract, or a promotion argument the moment you need it.

**Action step — do this today:** Block 45 minutes. Open your tracker and audit Q1. For every "Done" project: open your brag doc (or create one — a new markdown file is fine) and fill in the six fields. What you did, when, the impact, the skills, the evidence. That's it. Q1 is closed. The record is open. Now push your tracker live if it isn't already — the accountability starts the moment it's public.

Ship the thing. Track the thing. Document why it mattered.
